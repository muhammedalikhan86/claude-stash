# Writing an executive brief for Shaw & Partners

The user's executives do not want the operational detail of a project. A brief that lists stage
dates, replaced manual steps, outstanding questions and rollout mechanics is writing for a project
board, not for them. The user put it plainly: *"execs dont care about any of the details you put in
there."*

What they do want is three things.

**That discovery is running, and where it stands.** A picture of the waves or stages, and which one
is live. Progress, not process.

**That the work is genuine improvement, not a lift-and-shift.** This is the point the user cares
about most and the one most easily lost. The team is not rebuilding the existing spreadsheet process
in software — discovery surfaced requirements the business had never written down, and the process
itself is being refined on the way through. Say what was *added* that nobody asked for, not only
what was automated. In the OFA initiative the standing example is the intake pipeline for newly
onboarded accounts, a gap where arrangements reached the register only because somebody typed them
there.

**That the guardrails are in place.** Executives sponsoring automation of a compliance obligation
want to know a machine has not been left to run it alone. Name the specific places a human still
decides: review steps kept deliberately manual, notices and emails checked by a person before they
go out, and client-facing wording supplied by the business rather than drafted by the tool.

Keep it short and keep the detail out. Dates belong in one small table at most; the questions,
the rollout steps and the before-and-after comparisons belong in the proposal documents, not here.
